A Brief Documented Audio History"

The founding

Dr. John Jefferson Smallwood — born enslaved in 1863 — purchased 65¾ acres on the James River in 1891 and opened the Institute on October 12, 1892, with fewer than ten pupils and less than fifty dollars in cash.

Lincoln Memorial Hall

Dedicated May 19–21, 1912 — a four‑story, $41,000 brick hall funded largely by Mrs. John Hay. Dr. Smallwood died four months later, on September 29, 1912, at age 49.

Garvey, the UNIA, and closure

In 1926 the Universal Negro Improvement Association bought the debt‑burdened campus and reopened it as Universal Liberty University. It never recovered from Garvey's imprisonment and the 1929 crash.

The revival

The campus was demolished by dynamite in 1941, but a planned cultural center in Claremont will carry the Institute's name and mission forward.

1619

First recorded arrival of enslaved Africans in Virginia

Claremont sits near the landing site historically associated with the first recorded arrival of enslaved Africans in English North America — a resonance later drawn out by the Institute's own founding on this ground.

The first recorded African captive sold into bondage in English North America is auctioned at Claremont, Virginia — on the very site Dr. Smallwood would purchase over two and a half centuries later for the Institute.

Sept. 19, 1863

John Jefferson Smallwood is born enslaved

Born on the cotton plantation of Marcus W. Smallwood in Rich Square, Northampton County, North Carolina, to Mary Eliza and David Jefferson Smallwood. At five months old he was sold away from his parents along with the rest of his family.

c. 1873–1884

Bound status, court challenge, and escape

By Smallwood's own 1888–90 account: around age 10 he learned he was considered "bound" to his former master until age 21; at 14 he unsuccessfully challenged this in court; after two failed escape attempts and whippings, he succeeded on a third attempt near age 21, walking 125 miles from Scotland Neck, N.C., to Franklin, Va.

Lincoln Hall built; John Hay Wharf constructed

Smallwood places the building contract with architect Carl Ruehrmund and contractor D. J. Farrar of Richmond. The John Hay Wharf is built in 1911 at a cost of $3,797, per the souvenir programme's own history.

May 19–21, 1912

Lincoln Memorial Hall dedicated

A three-day dedication, presided over by Gov. William Hodges Mann, marks the completion of the Institute's largest building — a four-story, 133½ by 49½ foot brick, cement, and stone hall costing $45,987 (plus $15,800–$20,000 for the power house, water plant, and sewerage), funded largely by Mrs. Clara S. Hay. Events include a Sunday consecration service and Baccalaureate sermon, Monday's formal dedication of the hall, electric plant, and John Hay Wharf, an oratorical contest for the William's Prize, a Farmers' Congress, and Tuesday's Commencement Exercises for the Class of 1912. The New York Times and Richmond Planet both cover the event. That same year, a General Electric generating plant begins supplying electricity to the school and the town of Claremont. The original souvenir programme survives — see the Dedication Programme tab.

May 25, 1912

School property valued at $750,200

Per the souvenir programme, the value of school property at the closing of the school year is recorded as $750,200 — a figure that would make the 1913 receivership crisis, just months later, especially striking.

"I have ruined my health..."

Fifteen days before his death, Smallwood writes: "I have ruined my health and my life trying to complete this work."

Dr. Smallwood dies, age 49

His death certificate lists "adhesion from appendicitis." Funeral services are held at Third Street A.M.E. Church in Richmond; his body is returned to the Institute by steamer for burial. Rosa Smallwood takes up leadership of the school.

Property placed in receivership

Leadership connected to the Corey Memorial Institute of Portsmouth, Virginia — affiliated with the Norfolk Union Baptist Association — arrives in Claremont as the Baptist General Association purchases the Smallwood Memorial Institute's campus.

Rechartered as Smallwood-Corey Memorial Institute

The school is rechartered as the Smallwood-Corey Memorial Industrial and Collegiate Institute.

Renamed again

Shortened to the Smallwood-Corey Industrial Institute.

c. 1925

Trustees appeal to the UNIA

Struggling against chronic debt, the Smallwood-Corey trustees appeal to Marcus Garvey's Universal Negro Improvement Association for financial help, through Rev. Dr. J. G. St. Clair Drake, who served simultaneously as the Institute's vice president and a UNIA organizer.

1926

The UNIA purchases the campus

The UNIA buys the Claremont property for $7,300 and assumes between $53,000 and $55,000 of existing debt. The campus — valued at roughly $250,000 — includes Lincoln Hall (valued at about $100,000), which the UNIA renames Garvey Hall. The school reopens in September 1926 as Universal Liberty University, with Professor Caleb Robinson as principal and Rev. Dr. St. Clair Drake as vice principal.

May 1927

First commencement

Universal Liberty University celebrates its first completed academic year, drawing UNIA leadership from around the country.

Oct. 1929

The university announces it will not reopen

Garvey's imprisonment and deportation fracture UNIA leadership into competing factions, and the 1929 stock market crash delivers a final blow. The Negro World announces the school will not reopen pending reorganization — and it never does.

1934

Campus found dilapidated

A former student and a former professor visit the abandoned campus and find the buildings already deteriorating.

May 1937

Property sold at auction for $100

The 65¾-acre property is sold at public auction to H. C. Redd of Hanover County, Virginia. Smallwood's memorial monument is relocated to nearby Abundant Life Christian Church.

June 1941

Remaining buildings demolished

The last campus buildings are demolished by controlled dynamite explosions, bringing a final end to the physical campus that began as Dr. Smallwood's 65¾-acre purchase fifty years earlier.

2010

Historical marker K-331 erected

The Virginia Department of Historical Resources erects marker K-331 at the intersection of Rt. 10 & Rt. 40, Colonial Trail West, Spring Grove, Virginia.

Today

A planned revival

A planned cultural center in Claremont will carry the Institute's name and mission forward, alongside this ongoing community archive project.

Founder: Dr. John Jefferson Smallwood

Enslaved as an infant, self‑taught as a child, and internationally known as an orator by his thirties — before founding one of Surry County's few schools for Black students.

John Jefferson Smallwood

Sept. 19, 1863 — Sept. 29, 1912 · Rich Square, N.C. → Claremont, Va.

Founder, president, and general financial agent of the Temperance Industrial and Collegiate Institute for its entire twenty‑year existence under his leadership, and — by the account of his own contemporaries — one of the most sought‑after Black orators in New England.

Early life and enslavement

John Jefferson Smallwood was born enslaved on September 19, 1863, on a cotton plantation in Rich Square, Northampton County, North Carolina, to Mary Eliza Smallwood and David Jefferson Smallwood. Family history, recorded in his descendant Mary E. C. Drew's biography Divine Will, Restless Heart, holds that his mother was the daughter of Nat Turner, the enslaved preacher who led the 1831 rebellion in Southampton County, Virginia. While he was still an infant, his parents and siblings were sold away — he later said he was only five months old.

Family tradition, not independently verified: the Nat Turner lineage is carried down through Smallwood family history and Drew's biography, but has not been confirmed through contemporary documentation consulted for this record.

The Butler School and self-education

Family‑history sources hold that in the final months of the Civil War, the plantation owner Marcus W. Smallwood was aided by the young John Jefferson during a confrontation with Union troops, and in gratitude arranged in October 1870 for him — at age seven — to attend the Butler School, a primary department of the Hampton Normal and Agricultural Institute (today Hampton University).

Unverified episode: this account is recounted in family‑history sources and has not been independently verified through contemporary documentation.

Marcus W. Smallwood died in June 1871. Without a parent, guardian, or financial support, seven‑year‑old John Jefferson was told not to return to the Butler School. Undeterred, he taught himself by reading every newspaper and public speech he could find, and later claimed to have read his copy of Webster's Old Spelling Book a hundred times over.

A differing first-person account: in an 1888–1890 newspaper interview (transcribed in full below), Smallwood himself dated his discovery of his "bound boy" status to around age 10, an unsuccessful court challenge to age 14, and his third and finally successful escape attempt to nearly age 21 — walking 125 miles from Scotland Neck, N.C., to Franklin, Va. This differs from the age‑seven account above drawn from later family‑history sources. Both are presented here rather than silently reconciled.

Education and life abroad

He went on to attend Hampton Institute, Shaw University, and Wesleyan Academy in Wilbraham, Massachusetts, and later lectured and studied abroad in England, France, and Germany — by some accounts conceiving the idea for his own school while studying at Trinity College, London. He earned a Ph.D. and was ordained a minister, and by the time he founded his school he was already recognized as one of the most eloquent African American orators in America and England. A contemporary newspaper interview described his oratory as "dignified and graceful," suggestive of Frederick Douglass, and quoted friends who believed that "in time he will surpass Douglass."

Founding the Institute

On August 11, 1891, Dr. Smallwood purchased 65¾ acres on the edge of Claremont, Virginia — a town with a port on the James River, situated midway between Richmond and Norfolk. There, on October 12, 1892, he opened the Temperance Industrial and Collegiate Institute with fewer than ten pupils and less than fifty dollars in cash, holding classes in a dining hall on the property. The school was formally chartered by the State of Virginia in July 1902.

The institution's earliest name — the Temperance Industrial and Collegiate Institute — reflects a curriculum that clearly integrated both vocational and classical courses. Its mission, as stated in its Circular of Information, was to:

"give its students a thorough mental, moral, industrial and religious training."

An 1906 advertisement in the Educational Section of the Richmond Times‑Dispatch described the same mission more pointedly — "For the Moral, Religious, Educational and Industrial Welfare of the Negro Youth" — and warned that:

"Smoking, Chewing Tobacco, Drinking Whiskey, Fighting, Laziness and Impure Living Will Not be Tolerated in Any Pupil."

Marriage and family

On September 20, 1900, Dr. Smallwood married twenty‑one‑year‑old Rosa E. Banks, daughter of Mat and Phyllis Banks of Richmond, Virginia. Rosa served as the Institute's Lady President and was described in the contemporary Black press as a brilliant essayist and a gifted scholar in her own right — in March 1901 she wrote directly to Vice President Theodore Roosevelt seeking emergency financial help for the school (see Archive). The couple had two children: a daughter, Thelma Evelyn Smallwood (1901–1948), and a son, John J. Smallwood Jr. (1906–1907), who died in infancy.

The Surry County Historical Society's published account states the couple had no children besides Thelma; genealogical records (FamilySearch) additionally list the son John J. Smallwood Jr., who lived less than a year. The two accounts are not necessarily in conflict, as a public‑facing summary may simply omit an infant death recorded in vital records.

Death and memorial

Dr. Smallwood died on September 29, 1912, at age 49 — roughly four months after Lincoln Memorial Hall's dedication. His official death certificate listed the cause as "adhesion from appendicitis," though his Claremont neighbors believed it was the result of his having poured every piece of himself into the school he built, a belief he himself seemed to anticipate, having written on September 14, 1912:

"I have ruined my health and my life trying to complete this work."

Funeral services were held at Third Street African Methodist Episcopal Church (now Bethel A.M.E.) in Richmond, with eulogies delivered by George R. Hovey, president of Virginia Union University, and John Mitchell Jr., editor of the Richmond Planet. His body was returned to the Institute by steamer for burial. His gravestone, erected by friends and patrons, is inscribed "Founder and President of the Temperance, Industrial and Collegiate Institute" and "My work is finished."

Read the full 1888–1890 newspaper interview: "Was Born a Slave"

Transcription of a contemporary newspaper interview, likely The Boston Globe, based on internal references to "The Globe." This is Smallwood's own first‑person account, containing biographical detail — including the name of the slave trader who separated his family (A. B. Frutrel) and his mother's purchaser (John B. Johnson of El Paso, Texas) — not found in any other source in this record. See the note above on where it differs from later family‑history accounts.

WAS BORN A SLAVE. Former Owner Still Claimed Him After War. Career of Brilliant Young Colored Orator. Triumphs Won by J. J. Smallwood in Face of Obstacles.

Probably the best known young colored man in New England today is Mr. John Jefferson Smallwood. For the past four years he has been upon the platform, giving lectures upon the American colored man. That he is successful as a lecturer and popular as an orator is apparent from the fact that wherever he is announced to lecture, the houses are invariably filled.

He was born a slave, and when only five months old was torn from the arms of his mother, who was sent eventually to El Paso, Tex. From that day he never saw her until about a year ago, when he discovered her whereabouts, after the manner resorted to by freedmen who since the war have sought to reunite their scattered families, by continuous advertising.

In the meantime, Mr. Smallwood endeavored to so educate himself as to be able to rightly set forth the wrongs perpetrated upon his race. Hence he can be called a self‑made man. But through the broader knowledge which cultivated intelligence brings, Mr. Smallwood has not stopped at the race question, but has entered upon the agitation of temperance and labor, topics affecting American citizens, white and colored.

His style of oratory, which is dignified and graceful, is suggestive of that of Hon. Frederic Douglass, and his friends, of whom he has a host, numbering among them some of the leading men and women in New England, say that in time he will surpass Douglass. His voice is very deep. In speaking he uses many gestures, and illustrates and emphasizes his argument with witty anecdotes. He stands about 5 feet 8 inches. He is well built, his shoulders are square and he has a well‑formed head. His complexion is brown and he has expressive brown eyes.

Wilbraham, Mass., is Mr. Smallwood's New England home. At present he is visiting the family of William A. Smith of Randolph, where a visit was made to him a few days ago. When told that a request had been made by many readers of The Globe for a sketch of his life he said:

"There are, indeed, in New England many friends of mine who are truly interested in my welfare. How different it is now from when I first set my feet on New England soil. I thought that liberty dwelt on every tree top, mountain and valley, that everywhere here a man was a man. But after nine months' residence in Massachusetts I awoke to the fact that the spirit of prejudice against one of my race was almost as deep here as in Virginia.

"My awakening came about in this manner. When I first went to Greenfield, Mass., I was employed by a publishing company, and was to receive a certain sum per day and my board for my services. After I had worked there about nine months my employer told me he had concluded that I was objectionable to white help in his employ on account of my color. I was disheartened at first, but I took my money and returned South to Virginia. Since I have become more widely known there are undoubtedly many up there who wish they had not objected. But, after all, they knew not what they did. For New England and her people, with but few exceptions, I can only speak in the highest praise.

"I was born Sept. 19, 1863, a slave upon the plantation of Hon. Marcus W. Smallwood of Northampton, N. C., who was at that time representative to Congress from the second congressional district. His plantation was the largest cotton plantation in eastern North Carolina and was worked by hundreds of slaves. It was 25 miles from a railroad and 10 miles from a steamboat landing, the nearest river being the Roanoke. This district now, by the way, is represented by a colored man, Hon. H. P. Cheatham.

"My parents' names were David Jefferson and Mary Elizabeth Smallwood. The slaves usually took the names of the masters upon whose plantations they worked. I am the youngest of eight children, five sisters and three brothers. When I was a babe 5 months and 10 days old, both of my parents were sold to a slave trader named A. B. Frutrel and carried to Birmingham, Ala., where they were separated. My mother was sold to John B. Johnson of El Paso, Tex. She never saw my father again. He was carried to Louisville, Ky., where he was afterward sold, and what became of him I have never learned, for I can trace him no farther.

"A little over a year ago I found my mother, who had with her two of my sisters at El Paso. Of the rest of our scattered family I have not yet learned the whereabouts. This is only an instance of the hundreds of colored families who through cruel slavery have become scattered. It is true that some have become reunited, but the majority never will meet their loved ones again.

"After my parents were taken away from me I was left on the plantation in the care of the 'mammies.' The war had closed and on every hand the wildest desolation prevailed. At the age of 10 years, after having been permitted to roam around the plantation at will, I found that my former master claimed that I was bound to him until I was 21. At the age of 14 years I questioned this right in court. It was decided that as my master was a man of undoubted integrity that right was unquestioned, and I must stay with him until I was 21 years old. Twice I attempted to run away, but was caught and rebuked with a whipping. The third time I attempted I was nearly 21 years old. This time I was successful, and walked from Scotland Neck, N. C., to Franklin, Va., a distance of 125 miles.

"When I was about 13 years old I accidentally got hold of an old Webster's spelling book. With this book, and in secret, I learned the alphabet, and then to form letters into words.

"While at Franklin I began a temperance movement, which assumed large proportions. At one of my meetings there was present a young man named William B. Davis of Malden, Mass. He came to me at the close of the meeting and said: 'Why don't you go North and educate yourself?' I replied that I had neither money, parents nor friends to help me, and that Bryant Farmer, my employer, had failed to pay me in cash and there was no way of my getting North.

"He then gave me the address of Ira G. Ross, pastor of the First M. E. church, Greenfield, Mass., and told me to write to that gentleman, as he was interested in the general welfare of my race. As I had learned to write during my leisure hours while at work, I wrote to Mr. Ross. He in reply told me to come to New England, and after learning my financial condition sent me $25. With this same sum I reached Greenfield, Mass. Dr. Crowell of Lynn, hearing how I was treated there, became my benefactor.

"In 1886 I entered Wilbraham Academy with $14 in my pocket. That same year I returned to Franklin and became engaged in the local option contest in the second congressional district of that State. I was put in nomination for Congress against Mahone, but declined the honor, until I have become an educated man. After being mobbed in Virginia, I returned North and resumed my studies, aiding myself by lecturing in various churches and other organizations throughout New England, lecturing upon the American…" [article continues beyond this clipping]

Campus & Curriculum

A self-sufficient working campus that paired classical, collegiate coursework with industrial and agricultural training — an unusual hybrid for its era.

From Claremont Manor to Claremont Colony

The land Dr. Smallwood eventually purchased had passed through several owners shortly before his arrival. William Orgain Allen, the first builder and owner of nearby Bacon's Castle, died in 1875, and his eldest son — also named William — settled in New York and relinquished the family's Claremont Manor holdings. Between 1879 and 1882, Joseph Franklin Mancha of Delaware — for whom Mancha Drive is named — purchased tracts of the former Claremont Manor estate from the Allen family for colonization purposes. The town of Claremont was formally incorporated on January 16, 1886, with ambitions of becoming a port city to rival Norfolk; though that larger ambition was never realized, remnants of the old railroad and boat piers survive today.

Buildings and infrastructure

By the time of Dr. Smallwood's death, the campus had grown to more than sixty‑five acres along the James River and encompassed fourteen buildings. Lincoln Memorial Hall, the Institute's largest academic building, was a four‑story brick structure appraised at $41,000 in 1912 and covered at the time by both The New York Times and the Richmond Planet. Bagley Hall stood among the residential and instructional buildings sited along the bluffs of the James River. Dr. Smallwood also installed a General Electric generating plant on campus in 1912, complete with street lights, poles, transformers, and meters — supplying electricity not only to the school but to the surrounding town of Claremont for years afterward. Because the Institute operated without public funding for its entire thirty‑six years, Dr. Smallwood personally carried the burden of keeping it financially afloat, traveling widely to lecture and raising substantial sums from donors.

Industrial department

Industrial and agricultural training formed a significant part of the Institute's program. The first major capital expenditure — funds raised by Smallwood through years of lecturing — went specifically toward the school farm. Treasurer Emmet Ellis wrote that "the institution has a good farm fronting the James River, upon which is cultivated corn, potatoes and vegetables." The campus operated as a largely self‑sufficient community, combining academic halls with working industrial and agricultural operations that fed, housed, and trained its students: a mattress‑making facility, a canning operation, a saw mill, a working farm, a shoemaking operation, a printing press producing the school's own newspaper, and dormitory rooms for resident students. An inventory of school property taken after Smallwood's death lists livestock, farm implements, and crops of peanuts and potatoes.

According to Ellis, the school existed "to teach the Negro self‑reliance, race pride and a practical trade, to establish…various industries, house cleaning and scientific cooking, general laundry work upon a business scale."

Collegiate department

A notable feature that distinguished Smallwood's school from strictly industrial schools like Hampton was its robust collegiate curriculum. The school offered three years of high‑school training in addition to four years of college coursework, with students required to take courses including English Literature, Natural Philosophy, and Political Economy. Students came not only from nearby Virginia towns but from as far away as New York City, Washington, D.C., and Louisville, Kentucky. The word "Normal" was conspicuously absent from the school's name, indicating that administrators chose not to emphasize teacher education as the school's primary purpose — even though some graduates did become teachers.

Governance and the Monthly Advocate

The Institute published its own school newspaper, The Monthly Advocate, printed on the campus's own press and giving students hands‑on training in typesetting alongside the school's other trades. The Institute was governed by a Board of Trustees drawn from Virginia, New York, and North Carolina, reflecting the geographic reach of Smallwood's fundraising network.

Fundraising and donors

Like most African American schools of the era, the Institute relied exclusively on the largesse of individual donors rather than a major benevolent organization. Smallwood lectured throughout the United States and abroad, writing countless fundraising letters to acquaintances and public figures, including John Milton Hay (former U.S. Ambassador to Great Britain and Secretary of State, and a financial donor whose wife funded Lincoln Memorial Hall), Henry Cook of Lenox, Massachusetts, Emily Howland, Catherine Peterson, Susan B. Anthony (who inscribed donated volumes to the Institute's library in 1896), and Elizabeth Cady Stanton and Frederick Douglass, among Smallwood's fundraising correspondents. The school was chronically in debt throughout its existence, and this financial pressure meant that donor preferences inevitably influenced its educational direction.

Historical significance

Historian Benjamin Swenson argues that the Institute "arguably forged a unique model of education by integrating distinct combinations of both academic and industrial training." Rather than strictly following the Hampton‑Tuskegee model of industrial education or the classical curriculum championed by W. E. B. Du Bois, Smallwood's school blended both — reflecting the broader, contested landscape of African American education in the post‑Reconstruction South. The location carried its own historical resonance: Claremont sits near the landing site associated with the first recorded arrival of enslaved Africans in Virginia in 1619, and Smallwood himself was, by family tradition, a direct descendant of Nat Turner — linking his project of Black education and uplift to the region's deepest histories of bondage and resistance.

Read the full transcription: Letter to Vice President Theodore Roosevelt (March 18, 1901)

Claremont, Va., March 18, 1901. Hon. Theodore Roosevelt, Vice Pres. U.S.A.

My dear Mr. Vice President:

You will excuse me please, for the seeming presumption, if I dare, honored Sir, to send you this letter. I am a Negro woman at the head of the Institution named above which, as you know was founded by my faithful husband, Oct. 12/92.

The Institution itself is an interesting piece of Virginia and National history founded upon moral, industrial, and religious lines, operated by a young man with double his will, in the face of White Caps, Ku‑Klux and shot guns. My husband planted this young, industrial, Scientific and classical Institution upon the very spot where his mother was sold as a slave and where his grandfather was shot because he would not allow his overseer to whip his wife in his presence.

No living soul can imagine what we have passed through here since '92. We owe $3500. It must be paid April 1, April 15, May 15th, respectively. We write to ask you to help us meet these obligations, please.

The letterhead lists the school year as nine months and fifteen days, board and tuition at $6.50, and a request for $10,000 and 65,000 feet of lumber to begin the new Industrial Hall — plus a saw mill, farm implements, and a printing press.

Sources & editorial notes

This archive combines an earlier community‑maintained record with a more recent, independently corroborated research compilation. Where the two conflicted, the better‑corroborated, more recent facts were used.

Editorial note on corrections

This record combines material from Benjamin Swenson's 2004 M.A. thesis with more recent, independently corroborated research (the VCU Libraries Social Welfare History Project, the Surry County Historical Society, Virginia DHR Marker K‑331, and Wikipedia). Where the two sets of sources conflicted, the better‑corroborated facts were used and the thesis's figures were superseded. Notable corrections include: the year of Dr. Smallwood's death (1912, not 1910, as confirmed by his death certificate, contemporary newspaper coverage, and the official historical marker); the school's eventual closure (the original Smallwood‑founded institution continued under new leadership and new names through the 1920s, ultimately becoming Universal Liberty University under the UNIA before folding in 1929 and the campus being sold and demolished in 1937–1941, rather than closing simply in 1928 following the 1921 Corey merger); and the framing of Smallwood's descent from Nat Turner, which multiple independent sources state more directly than the thesis's framing as merely "claimed." Two details drawn from family‑history materials — the account of a young Smallwood intervening on Marcus W. Smallwood's behalf during a Civil War‑era confrontation, and certain round‑number statistics regarding lecture mileage and total donations — rest on family tradition and have not been independently verified.
